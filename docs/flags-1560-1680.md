# Flags at sea and ashore in the Caribbean, 1560–1680

Reference for every flag the game shows: the sighted ship's colours
(`sail-colours`, `colourFlag` in `src/vignettes.mjs`, the flag sheet in
`tools/build_colours.py`), the town and fort flags of the harbour, quay and
assault pictures (`FORT_FLAGS` in `tools/build_vignette.py`), and any flag a
vignette prompt describes (`docs/scene-vignettes.md`). It covers the four
nations of the game (Spain, England, France, Holland), the buccaneers and
pirates, and the other powers a ship could meet, for the game's six eras:
1560, 1600, 1620, 1640, 1660 and 1680.

Researched on 2026-11-18 from vexillological and period sources, listed at
the end of each section. This is a working document, not a final authority:
the items marked *uncertain* or *unknown* should be checked against the
printed works named in "Not yet verified" before anyone treats them as
settled.

## How to read this guide

**Confidence.** Each statement carries one of these labels:

- **confirmed**: a period text, or a scholarly work that quotes one.
- **probable**: a vexillological reference (mostly Flags of the World) or
  several secondary sources that agree, without a primary text seen.
- **uncertain**: one secondary source, or sources that disagree.
- **unknown**: no evidence found. The game may still need a choice; the
  "Game choice" lines say what the smallest plausible one is.

**Roles.** A ship of this period could fly several flags at once:

- the **ensign**, at the stern on its staff: the flag a lookout reads as
  "her colours", and the one this guide gives first;
- the **jack**, a small flag on the bowsprit;
- **masthead flags** and long **pendants** (streamers), often the oldest
  national flag of the nation, kept there after the stern ensign changed.

Ships are sorted into four roles: the **navy** (royal or state warships and
their escorts), **merchants**, **privateers** (private ships under a letter
of marque or commission), and **forts and towns**.

**Drawing.** Flag descriptions are written to be drawn in the game's 72 × 48
cell (3 : 2). Seventeenth-century ensigns were nearer 5 : 4 (Perrin), so
3 : 2 is a compromise. Colours were not standardised in this period; the hex
values are rendering suggestions, not historical specifications.

## Flag catalogue

### Spain

- **ES-B, Cross of Burgundy.** White field, red ragged saltire (two crossed
  knotted branches), arms about 6 px thick, corner to corner or couped short
  of the edges (both forms existed). The generic Spanish flag of the period
  at sea and on forts. *probable*
- **ES-A, white with the royal arms.** White field, the crowned Habsburg
  royal arms centred (not shifted to the hoist before 1701), about 20 × 24
  px: a quartered shield with red-and-gold Castile and white-and-purple León
  dominant, Aragon's gold-and-red pales, Austria and Burgundy below, a gold
  crown on top; the Golden Fleece collar round it can be left out at this
  size. The project's `spain-arms` is this flag simplified.
- **ES-R, royal standard.** Crimson (about `#9B111E`) field, the royal arms
  centred, a gold fringe (about 2 px). Square or swallow-tailed. Flown only
  by the *capitana* (the flagship) and sometimes the *almiranta* (the
  second flagship). *probable*
- **ES-T, galleons' tricolour.** Three horizontal stripes, red over white
  over yellow, the white one wider (12 / 24 / 12 px); in the white stripe a
  black crowned single-headed eagle in a gold collar, or nothing. Flown by
  the Indies galleons (*Armada de la Guarda de la Carrera*) from 1588 to
  about 1701. *probable*
- **ES-G, gallardete.** Very long, narrow streamer (about 72 × 6 px,
  tapering) in red and yellow. Masthead only. *probable* for the colours;
  the layout is not described.

### England

- **EN-G, St George's flag.** White field, red upright cross about 8 px
  wide. *confirmed*
- **EN-S, Elizabethan striped ensign.** Horizontal stripes with EN-G in the
  canton (the top hoist quarter, about 36 × 24 px). The stripe colours
  varied from ship to ship: green and white (the Tudor colours, attested
  for a squadron flag at Cadiz in 1596), red-white-blue, red-green-blue
  (Baffin's map of 1615). Some stripes were diagonal. There was no single
  pattern. *confirmed* that they existed; any one scheme is *probable*.
- **EN-R, red ensign.** Red field, EN-G in the canton. *confirmed*
- **EN-W and EN-B, white and blue ensigns.** As EN-R on a white or blue
  field (before 1702 the white ensign had no cross across its fly). The
  navy's squadron ensigns from 1653. *confirmed*
- **EN-U, the 1606 Union flag.** Blue field, white saltire about 8 px, red
  upright cross about 8 px with a white edge of about 2 px. No red saltire
  (that came in 1801). Flown at the main masthead and, small, as the jack.
  *confirmed*
- **EN-CJ, Commonwealth jack (1649–1658, 1659–1660).** Hoist half: EN-G;
  fly half: a gold harp on blue. *confirmed*
- **EN-PJ, Protectorate jack (May 1658–1659).** EN-U with a gold harp on a
  blue escutcheon at the centre. *probable* (the escutcheon)

### France

- **FR-W, plain white.** White field, no emblem. *confirmed* from 1643.
- **FR-C, the "old flag of the French nation".** Blue (about `#1F3F8F`)
  field, white upright cross about 8 px wide, centred. *confirmed* for 1643.
- **FR-CA, merchant flag of the 1661 ordinance.** FR-C with the crowned
  royal shield (blue with three gold fleurs-de-lis, about 14 × 16 px, a gold
  closed crown about 14 × 4 px) over the crossing. *confirmed* in law.
- **FR-L, bannière de France.** Blue field with gold fleurs-de-lis (three,
  or sown all over), often a swallow-tailed *étendard* (the fly cut about
  16 px deep). *probable* for forts and *uncertain* for royal ships before
  about 1600.
- **FR-CO, Compagnie des Indes occidentales (1664–1674).** White with the
  royal arms of France centred (the company's charter, art. 27). *confirmed*
  in the charter; its use in the Caribbean is *unknown*.

### Holland

- **NL-P, Prince's flag.** Three equal horizontal stripes, orange (about
  `#E8781E`) over white over blue (about `#21468B`), 16 px each. *confirmed*
- **NL-S, States flag.** As NL-P with red (about `#AE1C28`) in place of
  orange. *confirmed*
- **NL-P2 / NL-S2, double and triple flags.** The same colours repeated:
  six stripes of 8 px, or nine of about 5 px. Common on merchantmen and as
  navy jacks and flag-officer flags. *probable* (merchant use), *confirmed*
  (navy jacks, 1650s–1670s, in Van de Velde paintings).
- **NL-WIC, West India Company.** NL-P or NL-S with a G.W.C. monogram
  (the letters interlaced) in the white stripe. *uncertain*: the design has
  no dated source, and pictures of Dutch Brazil mostly show the plain
  tricolour.
- **NL-K, Zeeland privateer "blood flag".** Plain red; at Vlissingen, a
  white jug (*jacobakannetje*) on red, about 20 × 28 px centred. *probable*
  for the late 17th century; dates are vague.

### Buccaneers and pirates

- **PI-R, the "bloody flag".** Plain red (about `#A01010`). The
  international signal for a fight without quarter, flown by navies as much
  as by buccaneers. *confirmed*
- **PI-K, company colours.** Red, green or red-and-yellow flags with
  personal devices, carried by buccaneer companies ashore (Ringrose, 1680:
  see below). *confirmed*

## Spain

### Evidence

**Two readings of the navy ensign.** Flags of the World (FOTW) gives the
Cross of Burgundy (ES-B) as the Spanish ensign from about 1520, replaced by
the white flag with the royal arms (ES-A) under Charles II (1665–1700). It
cites no primary source and no year for that change. A contributor on the
same page asks whether ES-A was used as an ensign before Charles II at all,
and the question is left open.

Hugo O'Donnell (Real Academia Matritense de Heráldica y Genealogía, 2002,
pp. 43–44) states the opposite chronology: "Desde finales del siglo XVI, el
color de la Escuadra de Castilla que integra a todas unidades y enseñas
españolas, es blanco con las armas reales, aunque se siguen usando banderas
tradicionales como secundarias." In his account the main ensign was white
with the royal arms from the late 16th century, the Cross of Burgundy and
other traditional flags staying in use as secondary flags, and the Bourbon
flag of 1701 changed only the arms. O'Donnell is the stronger source (a
naval historian, an academic lecture with references); FOTW is the more
widely repeated one.

Paintings support both flags in the middle of the period. In Van Soest's
*Battle of the Downs, 1639*, the flagship *Santiago* flies ES-B (dealer's
catalogue description). A painting of 1640 by Loef reportedly shows both
(a blog source; title and museum not found). *uncertain*

**Both readings agree on 1680:** ES-A at the stern of the navy, ES-B as the
jack and on forts.

**Royal standard.** O'Donnell: the royal standard was crimson, with or
without a gold fringe, flown only by the *capitana* and sometimes the
*almiranta*; it stayed crimson until the Bourbons made it white. A
Real Biblioteca document (date uncertain) lists a crimson damask royal
standard at the mainmast and the stern, ten banners at the bow, and gold
streamers. *probable*

**The Indies galleons.** In 1588 the galleon *San Martín* was issued 84
linen flags "blanco, amarillo y colorado" (O'Donnell 1999, via FOTW). The
Real Instrucción of 1647 gave the *América* squadron the red-white-yellow
flag "with the eagle and crown" (ES-T), and the *Flandes* squadron the same
stripes with the Cross of Burgundy in the white stripe. FOTW gives the end of
ES-T as about 1701. *probable*

**In 1560**, O'Donnell describes stern flags as "generally red with
religious symbols". The ensign as a fixed national flag was not yet
established. *uncertain* for the details.

**Merchants.** No source describes Spanish merchant colours before 1701:
the rule "red saltire on white for merchants" dates from the Bourbon
regulations. Siegel's 1912 tables (via FOTW) call the red-white-yellow
tricolour a "civil/customary" ensign. *unknown*; ES-B is the likeliest.

**Privateers.** Spain did not commission privateers in the Americas for
most of the period. The *guardacostas* begin with the *Ordenanza de corso*
of 1674 (the Cuban corso cédula is from 1680). Their flags are *unknown*;
ES-B or ES-A are plausible. The Spanish *corsario* Mateo Guarín sailed under
French colours at Nipe in 1685 (Little, 2023).

**Forts.** A naval-fortress Cross of Burgundy of the 16th–17th century
survives at the Chapultepec Castle Museum in Mexico (FOTW, citing Manzano
1997). No period evidence was found for Havana, Cartagena or San Juan
specifically; the cross flown at San Felipe del Morro today is a heritage
display. ES-B on every Spanish fort is *probable*.

**Colour variants.** The Burgundy saltire also appears red on yellow
(Spanish navy about 1547, Fernández Gaytán), white on blue, and red on
purple. These are minor variants; ES-B (red on white) is the form to use.
No evidence was found for a Habsburg double-eagle ensign after 1556, nor for
a naval "bandera morada" (purple flag) before 1700: the purple flags of the
*Armada de Barlovento* are land colours of 1731.

**Combat flag.** Perrin, citing Cleirac, reports that the Spanish flag of
combat was blue in the West Indies and red with the arms of Castile in
European waters. One secondhand statement: *uncertain*. Ringrose (1681)
saw Spanish men-of-war hoist a "bloody flag" off Juan Fernández (see
Caribbean practice).

## England

### Evidence

The main source is W. G. Perrin, *British Flags* (Cambridge University
Press, 1922). Perrin was the Admiralty Librarian and quotes the
proclamations of 1606, 1634 and 1674 and the Council of State orders in
full.

**1560: no stern ensign yet.** Ships flew St George's flag (EN-G) at the
masthead, long green-and-white streamers with a St George cross at the
hoist (as in the Anthony Roll of 1546), and royal-arms banners on
flagships. Ensigns first appear in naval stores in 1574. Perrin: ships not
belonging to the king "flew the flag of St George when they flew any flag
at all". *confirmed*

**1600: striped ensigns.** Stern ensigns were striped with a St George
canton (EN-S), the colours varying by ship (Perrin, plate IX). The East
India Company's red-and-white striped ensign with a St George canton was
"probably adopted on its formation" in 1600 and kept after 1676 by
tolerance. *confirmed* (stripes), *probable* (any given scheme)

**1606: the Union flag.** The proclamation of 12 April 1606 gave all ships
of England and Scotland the Union flag (EN-U) at the main, with their own
cross (St George or St Andrew) at the fore. *confirmed*

**1621–1625: the red ensign.** The first red ensign was made in 1621 (it
cost £4 16s). In July 1625 Sir F. Stewart proposed "a red ancient [for]
every one of the King's ships", adopted for the Cadiz fleet that October.
Inventories of 1626 still list old striped ensigns. *confirmed*. (The
project's current note "red ensign by 1620" is about five years early.)

**1634: the Union reserved.** The proclamation of 5 May 1634 reserved the
Union flag "for Our owne Ships and Ships in Our immediate Service";
merchants were to wear St George's cross (English) or St Andrew's
(Scottish). The jack (the Union in small, at the bowsprit) is known from
1633. Squadron pendants in red, white or blue date from 1625. *confirmed*

**Merchants and the red ensign.** Merchants adopted the red ensign
gradually: a record of 1656 of "English Collers" worn "with the Cross
downewards" proves a canton ensign by then. The proclamation of 18
September 1674 made it legal, as the colours "usually heretofore worn on
merchants' ships": "the Flag and Jack White, with a Red Cross" and "the
Ensign Red, with a like Cross in a Canton White". Merchants wearing the
king's colours or Union jack were punished (proclamations of 1661 and 1674,
warrant of 1666). *confirmed*

**Commonwealth and Protectorate (1649–1660).**

- 22 February 1649: St George's flag at the masthead.
- March–April 1649: the jack EN-CJ (St George in the hoist, a gold harp on
  blue in the fly).
- March 1653: squadron ensigns red, white and blue, each with "red crosse in
  chief" (a St George canton), in that order of precedence.
- 18 May 1658: the jack EN-PJ (the 1606 Union with a harp at the centre);
  in 1659 it reverted to EN-CJ.
- 13 May 1660 (the Restoration): "the Harp must be taken out of all their
  flags"; the 1606 Union flag and jack returned. The squadron ensigns
  continued.

*confirmed*

**1680.** Navy: the Union flag and jack; red, white or blue squadron
ensigns with a St George canton; pendants; flag officers' plain red, white
or blue flags at the main, fore or mizzen. Merchants: EN-R, EN-G and a
white-and-red-cross jack. *confirmed*

**Privateers.** No distinctive English privateer flag existed before 1694.
Perrin: "all privateers in the 17th century were in the habit of wearing
the Union jack", "more often without" permission. Under the Commonwealth,
letter-of-marque ships wore the Parliament jack (the *Helena*, 1652:
"the Parliament Jack on the bowsprit end and the English ensign on the
poop"). The red jack with the Union in the canton (the "Budgee jack",
noted by Pepys about 1687) became the privateers' jack by the proclamation
of 12 July 1694, after the game's eras. *confirmed*

**Elizabethan sea dogs.** Drake and Hawkins drew their flags from stock in
1594: royal-arms flags, St George flags, streamers and ensigns. Drake flew
a striped flag on the bowsprit in 1595–96. Nothing distinctive. The "black
banners" at Cartagena in 1585 are a disputed tradition (see Caribbean
practice). *confirmed* (no distinct flag)

**Forts and colonies.** No source was found for the flags over Barbados,
Bermuda, St Kitts or Jamaica forts before 1680. Exquemelin's English
edition (1684) has Morgan's men "setting up the English [colours]" on
captured castles (Portobelo 1668, Chagres 1671) without describing them.
The only colonial fort evidence is Boston's Castle Island: a red ensign
with a St George canton from 1636 (Furlong & McCandless, 1981). *unknown*
for the Caribbean; EN-G or EN-R is the plausible choice.

## France

### Evidence

**1560.** No source describes a navy ensign. The *bannière de France*
(FR-L, blue with gold fleurs-de-lis, often a swallow-tailed *étendard*) is
the royal flag of the period (Desjardins and Pinoteau, via Vachon). The
view of the Huguenot Fort Caroline in Florida (1564) shows it with the
royal arms. Merchants: the white cross on blue (FR-C) is *probable*; an
earlier variant, a white couped cross on red, appears on the Desceliers map
of 1546. *uncertain*

**Plain white at sea.** White is attested on French ships well before it
became law:

- Lescarbot, 1606, on the merchant ship *Jonas*: a "bannière blanche… à la
  pointe du mât".
- Le Jeune, 1632: "pavillons blancs sur les mâts de nos vaisseaux".
- Georges Fournier, *Hydrographie*, 1643, pp. 795–796: France flies
  "d'argent, ou blanc, sans aucun blazon, pour l'ordinaire", and "la Marine
  de commerce porte le pavillon bleu à croix blanche".

FOTW's date of 1638 for the white ensign rests on Danckert's chart of about
1700, which cannot date a beginning. The earliest year at which the navy
specifically flew plain white is *unknown*; the usage was established by
the 1630s–1640s.

**The 1661 ordinance** (9 October 1661) reserved white to the king's ships.
Merchants "arborent seulement l'ancien pavillon de la nation française, qui
est la croix blanche dans un étendard d'étoffe bleue, avec l'écu des armes
de Sa Majesté sur le tout" (FR-CA; text via Vachon, citing Sepet 1873 and
Pinoteau 2003). An ordinance of 12 July 1670 repeats the blue stern ensign
with a white cross (Mioque). *confirmed* in law. In practice, the plain
cross on blue and the illegal plain white are the well-documented merchant
flags; Vachon found no evidence that ships bound for Canada flew the armed
version.

**1680s.** The Ordonnance de la Marine of August 1681 and the ordinance of
15 April 1689: the king's ships fly no flag, pendant or ensign but white.
The admiral flies a square white flag at the main, the vice-admiral at the
fore, a *chef d'escadre* a white cornette at the mizzen, a captain
commanding more than one ship a white *flamme* (pendant) at the main
(1681, livre I, titre I, art. 7; *Encyclopédie*, citing the 1670 and 1689
ordinances). The 1689 merchant rule adds the collars of Saint-Michel and
Saint-Esprit round the arms, and allows other marks "pourvu que leur
enseigne de poupe ne soit point entièrement blanche". *confirmed*

**Regional merchant flags** were common and legal: Provence white with a
blue cross (Brémond's portulan, 1664); Calais a white cross on blue;
Normandy per pale white and blue; Brittany a black cross on white; Danckert
(about 1700) shows seven white and blue horizontal stripes. Saint-Malo and
Dieppe flags of the period are *unknown*. *uncertain* (secondary sources)

**Privateers and flibustiers.** The 1681 ordinance (livre III, titre IX,
art. 5): a ship "combattant sous autre pavillon que celui de l'État dont il
a commission" is good prize and its officers are punished as pirates; art. 3
forbids foreign commissions. No dedicated privateer flag was found. A red
flibustier flag is *unknown* before 1687 (see Caribbean practice). For the
Huguenot raiders of the 1560s, a Cuban secondary source (Roig de
Leuchsenring, 1929) says Jacques de Sores covered his guns with "the flag
of France" at Havana in 1555, colour not stated. *unknown*

**Companies.** The 1635 Compagnie des Îles d'Amérique flag is *unknown*.
The Compagnie des Indes occidentales (1664) was granted "le pavillon blanc
avec les armes de France" (charter, art. 27). *confirmed* in the charter.

**Forts.** Forts under the Marine in New France flew plain white, the flags
sent from France (Plaisance 1687, Acadia 1692 and 1695; Chartrand via
Vachon). No source was found for Martinique, Guadeloupe, Tortuga,
Saint-Domingue or Cayenne. White is *probable* by analogy, from about 1640.
Before that, the *bannière de France* is attested on French forts in North
America (Sainte-Croix 1604, Quebec 1608). The modern Martinique flag (blue,
white cross, four serpents) dates from 1766, after the period.

## Holland

### Evidence

**1560: the Habsburg Low Countries.** There was no Dutch state. Ships flew
the Cross of Burgundy (ES-B), "mostly at sea" under Spanish rule (FOTW).
*probable*. The Sea Beggars (*Watergeuzen*) appear from 1568; whether they
flew orange-white-blue at Den Briel in 1572 is *uncertain* (Poels: the
Geuzen adopted the colours "somewhat later in the 1570s"; the oldest colour
image is from 1575).

**Orange to red.** The sources agree on a gradual change:

- de Waard (1900, via Wikipedia): orange only 1588–1630, both 1630–1662,
  red only from 1663.
- Poels (2011): red appears around 1630 and has replaced orange by around
  1660.
- FOTW (Sensen): after 1660 the orange version is "very rare".
- Red-white-blue is first mentioned in 1596 or 1597.
- de Jonge (1831) reports an orange ban in 1652; no other source backs it.
  *uncertain*
- In 1664 a States-General resolution named the red flag the "Statenvlag"
  (it had been the "Hollandsche vlag"); only the name changed.

The cause (a dye that turned orange to red, or the House of Orange's loss
of favour) is *unknown*. Both colours are defensible for 1640.

**Navy.** An admiral flew the striped national flag at the main, a
vice-admiral at the fore, a rear-admiral at the mizzen; pendants marked the
squadron (Van de Velde, *The Gouden Leeuw at the Battle of the Texel*,
showing 1673, RMG BHC0315). Navy jacks in the 1650s–1660s were striped
red-white-blue, sometimes nine stripes (RMG BHC0910, about 1658; BHC0887),
or a city jack (a green-and-white Rotterdam jack in a copy of BHC0887).
The gyronny "geus" jack is called "traditional" by FOTW but is not seen in
these paintings; for 1560–1680 the striped jack is the safer choice.
*confirmed* for the 1650s–1670s.

**Merchants** flew the same national flags, often doubled or tripled
(NL-P2 / NL-S2). *probable*

**West India Company (1621; refounded 1674–1675).** FOTW (Sensen, citing an
article on 50 WIC colours in *Spiegel Historiael*, April 1975) gives the
tricolour with a G.W.C. monogram in the white stripe; variants join a small
"v" to the W (forming an A for the Amsterdam chamber). The letter colour is
not given. Ferreira (2019) finds that most images from Dutch Brazil show
plain tricolours. The gold monogram with a crown is Johan Maurits's Brazil
flag, not a general company flag. *uncertain*; the plain national flag is
*probable* for most WIC ships.

**Privateers (*kapers*).** Zeeland privateers flew a red "blood flag"; at
Vlissingen a white jug on red. One source places it at the mizzen, with
the Prince's pendant at the main and the city flag forward (a search
summary, not read in full). A manuscript of 1667 shows a white crowned
bottle on a red-white-blue flag (Sierksma 1962, via nl.wikipedia).
*probable* for the late 17th century, dates vague.

**Forts** (Curaçao from 1634, St. Eustatius, Essequibo, Berbice): no
source found. *unknown*; the plain national flag of the era (or the WIC
version) is the plausible choice.

## Caribbean practice

### Buccaneers sailed under commission or national colours

Buccaneers mostly flew the colours of the nation that commissioned them, or
of their own nation. Morgan's articles for Panama (1670) pay 50 pieces of
eight to the man first "taking down the Spanish colours and setting up the
English" (Exquemelin, Stallybrass edition, about p. 170); at Gibraltar the
pirates "pulled down the Spanish colours, and set up their own" (p. 97).
Haring: buccaneers took "little account… of the flag under which they
sailed". In 1681 Indians at Golfo Dulce had been told by a Spanish priest
to avoid "any ship nor vessel that had red colours, forasmuch as that they
were Englishmen" (Ringrose, p. 426). *confirmed*

Commissions came from wherever they could be had:

- Portugal: Mansfield's men sacked Sancti Spíritus in 1666 "on the strength
  of Portuguese commissions" (Haring, citing the Calendar of State Papers,
  Colonial). *confirmed*
- France: Jamaican crews took commissions from the governors of Tortuga or
  Petit-Goâve after the Treaty of Madrid (1670). *confirmed*
- Denmark: the governor of St Thomas harboured Jean Hamlin in 1682–1683.
  *confirmed*; a Danish commission is *unverified*.
- Courland: *unknown*.

### The red "bloody flag"

A plain red flag was the old "custom of the sea" signal for a fight without
quarter, going back to the Norman red *baucans* of about 1299 ("mort sans
remede"); Boteler (about 1634) describes the admiral hanging out "another
all redd, which is tearmed the bloudy Colors", and England put it in its
fighting instructions in 1647 (Perrin, pp. 159–161). *confirmed*

It was not a pirate flag. Off Juan Fernández in January 1681, three Spanish
men-of-war "instantly put out their bloody flags, and we… did the same with
ours" (Ringrose, pp. 399–400). *confirmed*

### Company colours ashore

At Santa Maria (Darien) in April 1680, Ringrose lists the buccaneer
companies' colours: Sharp, "a red flag, with a bunch of white and green
ribbons"; Sawkins, "a red flag striped with yellow"; Harris, "two green
flags"; Coxon, "a red flag" each; Cook, "red colours striped with yellow,
with a hand and sword for his device" (p. 300). These are land colours, not
ship ensigns, but they are the closest documented model for a captain's own
flag before 1700. *confirmed*

### Skull and crossbones: after the game's eras

- Exquemelin (1678 Dutch, 1684 English) describes no pirate emblem flag:
  no black flag, no skull. *confirmed* for the Stallybrass edition.
- Barbary corsairs off Cornwall in 1625 flew "a human skull on a dark
  green background" (Giles Milton, *White Gold*, p. 9, via FOTW).
  *probable*; not Caribbean.
- Drake's "black banners and streamers" at Cartagena in 1585 (Cordingly,
  via FOTW): historicity questioned. *uncertain*
- French flibustiers at Acaponeta (Pacific coast of Mexico), 1687–1688:
  a red flag with a white skull and crossed bones, the *pavillon sans
  quartier*, used on land. Benerson Little calls it "the only known instance
  of buccaneers flying the skull and bones"; Wikipedia cites a BnF logbook
  entry of 6 December 1687. *probable*
- Emanuel Wynne, July 1700, off the Cape Verde islands: a "sable ensign with
  cross bones, a death's head, and an hour glass" (Capt. John Cranby of HMS
  *Poole*, via Peter Earle, *The Pirate Wars*, p. 154). *probable*
- "Jolie rouge" as the origin of "Jolly Roger" has no period source and is
  a popular myth; "Old Roger" is attested in 1723.

A black skull-and-crossbones flag is therefore anachronistic for every
game era. The documented "pirate" flags up to 1680 are national or
commission colours, the plain red flag, and company colours.

### False colours

Approaching under another nation's flag was common in the Caribbean. *confirmed*

- Pierre François at the Rancherías pearl fleet, "putting forth the Spanish
  colours" (Exquemelin, pp. 63–64).
- The Spanish garrison of Santa Catalina in 1671 "put forth the English
  colours" to decoy an English ship (p. 123).
- Morgan's fire-ship at Maracaibo in 1669 flew "the English colours" to
  pass for a man-of-war (p. 162).
- Morgan's garrison at Chagres "put forth Spanish colours, thereby to allure
  and deceive".
- At Veracruz in 1683 the attackers "displayed the Spanish colours"; Thomas
  Pain landed at St Augustine in 1683 "under French colours" (Haring).
- A Spanish flyboat met Dudley's voyage of 1594–1595 under an English flag
  (Perrin, p. 198).

**Legality.** Perrin (1922): "There is no clear pronouncement of
International Law as to what is and what is not lawful in the use of false
colours." The modern rule (hoist true colours before opening fire) cannot
be traced to a 16th- or 17th-century text. *unknown* before 1680. But in
1675–1676 the Jamaica authorities charged the buccaneer John Deane with
"wearing Dutch, French and Spanish colours without commission" (Haring);
the French ordinance of 1681 makes fighting under a flag other than one's
commission's a pirate's act.

### Truce, parley and surrender

- The white flag of truce at sea dates from about 1500 (Perrin). In 1594
  Richard Hawkins's captain "caused a flagge of truce to be put in place of
  our Ensigne, and began to parley of our surrendering". John Smith (1626):
  "hang out a flag of truce… abase or take in his flag, strike their
  sailes". *confirmed*
- In the Caribbean: Spaniards "hang forth a white flag, in token of truce"
  (Exquemelin, p. 107); Morgan and the governor of Santa Catalina exchanged
  canoes under "colours of truce" and "white colours" in 1670; Ringrose
  records flags of truce in 1680–1681 (pp. 272, 378). *confirmed*
- Surrender: striking (lowering) the colours, and, as a stronger
  submission, striking the topsails. By 1666 lowering the colours alone
  sufficed. *confirmed*
- Once France made plain white its navy's ensign (1661), a white flag
  could be ambiguous (Perrin).

### "No peace beyond the line"

Garrett Mattingly (1963) traces the phrase to J. A. Williamson (1949) and
describes a verbal understanding at Cateau-Cambrésis in 1559: beyond the
lines (the prime meridian through Ferro and the Tropic of Cancer) the
treaty was disregarded. *probable* (abstract read only). No source ties it to
a flag rule; its effect was on commissions, not flags. Exquemelin records
the English king's "constant answer" that he "never gave any letters-patent
nor commissions" against Spain (p. 162).

### The English salute

England's claim that foreign ships strike their topsail, and after 1654
their flag, to English warships applied in the "Narrow Seas" (later as far
as Cape Finisterre). No evidence was found that it was enforced in the
Caribbean. *unknown*
