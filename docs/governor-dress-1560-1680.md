# Clothing of colonial governors on the Caribbean and northern South American coast, 1560–1680

Reference for the governor's portrait (`governor` vignette), which changes with
the era and the town's nation. The last section maps each era and nation to
the period painting the game pixelates (`tools/build_governor_portraits.py`,
sources in `assets/vignettes/PROVENANCE.md`).

## Scope and how to read this guide

This guide compares the clothing a Spanish, French, English, or Dutch senior
colonial official might plausibly have worn in the Caribbean and on the
northern coast of South America in 1560, 1600, 1620, 1640, 1660, and 1680.

It is a reconstruction from metropolitan dress history and surviving
portraits, not a claim that a standardised “tropical governor's uniform”
existed. Governors generally wore versions of elite civilian dress from their
home country. In hot, humid colonies, a person might choose lighter linen,
less heavy outerwear, and simpler garments for daily work, while retaining
richer formal clothing for ceremonies, audiences, and portraits.

**Chronological caveat:** for some powers and dates there was no settled
colony or colonial governorship on this coast. In those cases, the clothing
below describes a plausible expedition leader, privateer, merchant or official
from that country, not a governor who is known to have held office there.

The visual references are portraits and costume plates from Europe that help
establish the cut and silhouette. They are not portraits of Caribbean
governors.

Dutch, French and English overseas possessions were often administered
through chartered companies or local directors and commanders as well as
governors. The title and authority of an official therefore varied by colony
and date.

## 1. Spanish

Spanish governors were drawn from a court culture in which formal male dress
became notably sober and dark in the seventeenth century. The most useful
visual marker is the change from the pleated ruff of the late sixteenth and
early seventeenth centuries to the rigid, projecting *golilla* collar
associated with the reign of Philip IV. A short cloak, sword and dark
doublet or outer garment are plausible for a senior official, but not every
official would have worn the full court outfit every day.

### 1560: late Renaissance, Philip II period

- Fitted *jubón* (doublet), usually with a shaped waist and fitted sleeves.
- Pleated linen *lechuguilla* (ruff), generally less enormous than some later court examples.
- Padded *calzas* or trunk hose, stockings and leather shoes.
- Short cloak or cape, belt and sword; a hat outdoors.
- Dark colours were already prestigious, but colour and textile variety remained available.

Colonial status: a plausible Spanish imperial official's dress. Spanish rule
was already firmly established in the Caribbean and mainland territories.

### 1600: late Habsburg Renaissance

- Fitted doublet and voluminous breeches; a conspicuous ruff remained possible.
- Black or very dark wool or velvet for formal display, with white linen at neck and cuffs.
- Short cloak, sword and hat.
- The silhouette is still recognisably late sixteenth-century, not yet the mature mid-century golilla look.

### 1620: transition to the austere Baroque silhouette

- Dark doublet and breeches, with a smaller ruff or a flatter falling collar depending on taste and date.
- The ruff was giving way to simpler, flatter neckwear; the golilla would become a strong Spanish marker in the following decades.
- Cloak and sword remain appropriate for a gentleman or official.

### 1640: mature Philip IV style

- Dark *jubón* or *ropilla* over a shirt, with knee-length *calzones*.
- Rigid white golilla (a stiff, projecting linen collar) rather than a large circular ruff.
- Black stockings, low shoes, and a short dark *ferreruelo* cloak.
- A sword and restrained insignia could mark office or rank.

### 1660: late Habsburg formal dress

- The black or dark doublet or ropilla and rigid golilla remain characteristic.
- Hair is longer than in the early 1640s in many elite portraits, but still generally less voluminous than the French full-bottomed wig fashion.
- The overall impression is controlled, sober and formal rather than flamboyant.

### 1680: late seventeenth-century Spanish style

- Ropilla or short outer garment, short cloak or ferreruelo, knee breeches and the golilla.
- Velvet and silk could be used for an elite official; the palette often remained dark, with white linen and orders or insignia providing contrast.
- French influence existed, but a Spanish governor should not automatically be dressed in a fully French justaucorps-and-wig ensemble.

Visual references: Diego Velázquez's portraits of Philip IV (dark court
palette; ruff-era against golilla-era styling); the Museo del Traje's
portrait of a Spanish gentleman, c. 1690 (ropilla, ferreruelo and rigid
golilla).

## 2. French

The French timeline must be treated carefully: there was no French Caribbean
governor in 1560 or 1600. French settlement on St Kitts dates from 1625;
Martinique and Guadeloupe were settled in 1635. The later French court
fashions, especially the 1660s petticoat breeches and the 1680s long coat,
should not be projected backwards.

### 1560: French Renaissance, but no French Caribbean governor

- A French expedition leader might wear a fitted *pourpoint* (doublet), a small ruff or standing collar, slashed sleeves and padded trunk hose.
- Stockings, broad-toed shoes, short cloak, sword and hat.
- Velvet, silk, embroidery and brighter colours were possible for a wealthy gentleman.

Colonial status: no French Caribbean colonial governorship. This is a French
gentleman or expedition commander's clothing, not a colonial governor's
uniform.

### 1600: Henri IV period

- Fitted doublet with a small ruff or a falling *col rabattu* collar.
- Full breeches, stockings, shoes, cloak and hat.
- Less rigidly Elizabethan than the 1560 silhouette; a softer falling collar is increasingly plausible.

Colonial status: no permanent French Caribbean administration at this date.

### 1620: Louis XIII period

- Pointed or close-fitting doublet, fuller sleeves and breeches.
- A falling linen collar with lace edging becomes increasingly fashionable.
- Stockings, decorative shoe bows; boots and a broad-brimmed hat for travel or riding.

Colonial status: the French settlement on St Kitts was established in 1625,
five years after this date.

### 1640: early Baroque French style

- Shorter doublet, falling lace collar, fuller breeches and decorative cuffs.
- Boots for riding and travel; shoes and stockings for formal indoor wear.
- Ribbons and richer fabrics are plausible, but avoid the most exaggerated 1660s court costume.

Colonial status: French settlements in Martinique and Guadeloupe were
established in 1635. French Guiana remained less secure.

### 1660: Louis XIV court fashion

- Short doublet or jacket, lace falling collar or emerging cravat, and conspicuously full *rhingrave* (petticoat) breeches.
- Ribbons, lace, long curled hair, stockings and shoes with bows.
- This is a decorative, high-fashion silhouette; a working colonial official might dress more simply.

### 1680: the long coat emerges

- Long *justaucorps* coat over a waistcoat (*veste*), with a cravat, knee breeches and stockings.
- Wig or long curled hair; shoes with buckles or bows.
- The three-piece male silhouette is emerging, but should not be treated as an already fixed modern suit.

Visual references: French court costume, 1577, from a sixteenth-century
costume engraving; Charles Grignion, *Habit of a French nobleman in 1660*
(the colourful Restoration-era French silhouette).

## 3. English

For the dates before permanent English settlement, the figure should be
described as an English explorer, privateer, merchant or expedition leader,
not a Caribbean colonial governor. “English” is the historically correct term
before the 1707 union that created Great Britain.

### 1560: Tudor style, no English Caribbean governor

- Fitted Tudor doublet, small ruff or standing collar, padded trunk hose.
- Stockings, broad-toed shoes, short cloak and feathered hat.
- A wealthy gentleman might wear slashing, embroidery and velvet; a sailor or privateer would be much more practical.

### 1600: late Elizabethan, early Stuart

- Fitted doublet with a ruff or falling collar.
- Trunk hose or longer breeches, stockings, cloak and sword.
- Richly embroidered clothing was possible for a high-status gentleman, but not a universal everyday outfit.

### 1620: James I period

- Pointed doublet, falling band or ruff, broad sleeves and full breeches.
- Stockings, rosettes or ribbons, broad-brimmed hat and cloak.
- Boots are plausible for travel and riding.

Colonial status: Bermuda had been settled from 1612; English settlement on
St Kitts began in 1623 and Barbados in 1627.

### 1640: Charles I, Cavalier period

- Fitted doublet, lace-edged falling band, knee-length breeches.
- Boots, cloak, sword and broad-brimmed hat.
- A wealthy royalist-style gentleman might wear rich fabric and lace; the style should not be confused with later Restoration dress.

### 1660: Restoration fashion

- Short doublet and strikingly full petticoat breeches (also called rhinegraves in some contexts).
- Lace cuffs, ribbons, long hair, stockings and shoes with bows.
- A distinctive, sometimes extravagant transitional fashion; a strong visual choice for a wealthy English official around 1660.

Colonial status: Jamaica had been captured in 1655, but Edward D'Oyley's
regular governorship began in 1661.

### 1680: late Stuart, Charles II period

- Long coat, waistcoat, cravat and knee breeches.
- Stockings, shoes and a wig or long curled hair.
- The silhouette increasingly resembles the late seventeenth-century European male suit, though details and materials vary.

Visual reference: George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham, in an early
seventeenth-century portrait (rich doublet, lace collar, breeches and cloak).
He was an elite courtier, not a colonial official.

## 4. Dutch

“Dutch” needs a political qualification. In 1560 the Low Countries were
still under Habsburg rule; the Dutch Republic emerged later. In the
seventeenth century, Dutch overseas possessions were frequently run by the
Dutch West India Company (WIC), local companies or appointed directors and
commanders. A Dutch official's clothing could look like that of a wealthy
merchant or civic regent rather than a hereditary aristocrat.

Dutch dress is often represented as uniformly black, but this is misleading.
Dark formal clothing was common among civic elites, while coloured silks and
other rich textiles also appeared, particularly among younger or wealthy men.
Fine white linen, lace, tailoring and accessories could signal status.

### 1560: Netherlandish Renaissance, not yet a Dutch colonial official

- Fitted doublet, small ruff, trunk hose, stockings and cloak.
- Wool, velvet, silk and linen; black, brown, red, green and blue were all possible.
- A hat and sword would be plausible for a gentleman or military figure.

Political status: the Low Countries were still under Habsburg rule; there was
no Dutch Republic or Dutch colonial governorship.

### 1600: Dutch Republic, late Renaissance

- Doublet and large pleated ruff, trunk hose or longer breeches.
- Dark, sober fabrics were common among wealthy civic elites, but colour was not absent.
- Cloak, hat, sword belt and fine linen.

Colonial status: Dutch maritime expansion was underway, but there was not yet
an established Dutch Caribbean colonial administration.

### 1620: early Dutch colonial activity

- Doublet becoming shorter and tighter; breeches becoming more prominent and lengthening towards the knee.
- Ruff or falling collar, tall boots, cloak or cape, sword belt and broad-brimmed hat.
- French fashion began to influence Dutch dress in the 1620s and 1630s.

Colonial status: Dutch settlement in Essequibo dates from about 1616; Berbice
was founded in 1627.

### 1640: Dutch Golden Age civic and merchant style

- Fitted doublet, flat linen collar (*platte kraag*) often edged with lace, knee breeches and stockings.
- Shoes or boots; cloak and broad-brimmed hat.
- Dark clothing is common in formal portraits, but bright colours and expensive silks remain plausible.

Colonial status: Curaçao was captured in 1634; the Dutch were also present
in Brazil and Guiana.

### 1660: Dutch elite dress under growing French influence

- Short jacket or doublet, breeches, white collar or emerging cravat, long hair.
- Generally less flamboyant than the French court's petticoat-breeches fashion, though wealthy Dutchmen adopted international trends.
- Hats remain important in some portrait conventions, but wigs become increasingly visible in later seventeenth-century portraits.

Colonial status: Dutch Brazil had been lost in 1654; Suriname was captured
from the English in 1667.

### 1680: international Baroque style, often restrained

- Long coat (justaucorps), waistcoat, cravat and knee breeches.
- Stockings, shoes and wig or curled hair.
- A wealthy company official might wear high-quality cloth and embroidery, but a dark, restrained palette remains a plausible choice.

Visual references: Anthonie Palamedesz, portrait of a gentleman, 1657 (dark
doublet, white lace collar and cuffs, long hair); attributed to Bartholomeus
van der Helst, portrait of a gentleman with a black slashed doublet and
orange sash, c. 1650 (Dutch elite dress was not invariably all black).

## Practical reconstruction notes for a Caribbean governor

1. **Do not invent a tropical uniform.** The strongest evidence is for European court, civic and military fashions. A governor's colonial wardrobe would be an adaptation of those fashions, not a separate standard uniform.
2. **Separate public dress from daily dress.** For a formal audience or portrait, use the richer metropolitan silhouette. For routine administration, a lighter shirt, less elaborate outer garment and simpler footwear are more plausible.
3. **Use linen intelligently.** White linen at the neck and cuffs is visually and historically useful across all four traditions. It also provides a clear status signal in portraits.
4. **Rank matters.** A governor with a wealthy household and access to imported cloth could wear velvet, silk, lace, embroidery, sword and insignia. A remote or financially constrained official might have a more worn and practical wardrobe.
5. **Avoid anachronisms.** Do not give a 1620 French official the 1680 justaucorps; do not give a 1640 Spanish official a large Elizabethan ruff; do not give a 1560 Dutch colonial governor a Dutch Republic identity; and do not treat Jamaica as an English colony before 1655.
6. **Treat portraits as evidence of elite ideals, not everyday reality.** Portraits show what a sitter wished to project. They are excellent for silhouette and details, but less reliable for what an official wore while travelling, working outdoors or enduring Caribbean heat.

**Final caution:** the most defensible reconstruction is a period-accurate
European elite outfit adapted to the practical conditions of a colonial
posting. The evidence does not support a precise, universal costume for “the
governor of a Caribbean town” in each year. Where no colony existed, the
guide describes a visitor or expedition figure instead.
